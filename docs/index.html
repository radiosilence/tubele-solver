TUBELE

Solving Tubele by version-space search on the London rail graph

Adaptive identification of one station among 420, with an exact consistency filter, a collision-entropy objective, counting lower bounds it nearly meets, and a comparison with 13,030 human games.

Inspired by Tubele. This report is about Tubele, a daily London station guessing game made by Mike Keefe. It began as an attempt to see how well a program could play his game using only what the game shows a player. Play the original at tubele.app, read Mike's account of how he built it, and see more of his work at mikekeefe.com.

Abstract Tubele asks a player to identify a hidden station on London's Underground, Overground, DLR and Elizabeth line networks in at most six guesses. Each guess is answered with the shortest-path distance in stops, a fare-zone comparison and the set of shared lines, and the game additionally reveals one of the answer's transport modes at the start and one further property after each wrong guess. We formalise the game as adaptive identification over a finite hypothesis space under a guess-dependent partition, build the network independently from TfL Open Data, and analyse a policy that maintains the exact version space and chooses each guess to minimise the expected size of the next one, which we show equals $|C|\,2^{-H_2}$ for the Rényi collision entropy $H_2$ of the induced partition. Over all (opener, answer) pairs the policy never needs more than five guesses on the map alone and averages from its best opener, within of a second-order counting lower bound for that opener; no policy can average below . With the progressive clues, the reference implementation averages 2.264 guesses with a worst case of four, and the re-implementation used for this report with a worst case of , against an information bound of . Players of the live game, who see the same clues, win 80.7% of daily games and 73.1% of practice games; fitted to a constant-information survival model they extract about bits per guess, against the bits needed.

Contributions

  1. A formal model of Tubele (§2) as identification under a deterministic feedback function with values in $\Phi = \mathbb{N}\times\{\textsf{exact},\textsf{near},\textsf{none}\}\times 2^{\Lambda}$, extended with revealed clue functions $\kappa_j$.
  2. An independent construction of the station graph from TfL Open Data that reproduces the game's diameter (§3), with a soft-consistency fallback for graph drift.
  3. A proof that the greedy objective is the collision probability of the feedback partition, $\mathbb{E}|C'| = |C|\,2^{-H_2} - [g\in C]/|C|$ (Theorem 1), and two lower bounds on expected guesses: an information bound and a sharper second-order counting bound (§4).
  4. Complete results for every opener, with and without clues, by mode and by line, and the full decision trees (§6).
  5. A comparison with 13,030 recorded human games, including a maximum-likelihood estimate of human information per guess (§7).
  6. A behavioural analysis of how people play (§8): seven hypotheses on anchoring, use of feedback, clues, familiarity, learning, effort and sharing, each tested against the game's aggregate data with a matched machine baseline.

1Introduction

Tubele is a daily puzzle in the family of Wordle: one hidden answer, a small budget of guesses, structured feedback after each. Its answer space is the 420 stations of four London rail networks and its feedback is geometric. A guess reports how many stops separate it from the answer along the network, whether its fare zone matches, and which of its lines also serve the answer. Unlike Wordle, whose feedback alphabet is fixed at $3^5$, Tubele's alphabet is induced by the graph: a guess's power to discriminate depends on where it sits, how many lines meet there, and how the network fans out around it. The question this report answers is how well a machine that knows only the map, and plays only through the game's own interface, can do, and how that compares with people.

The game is Mike Keefe's, and the problem studied here exists because of one of his design decisions. As he describes in his write-up of the build, he rejected the compass bearing and straight-line distance that similar games report, judging them useless to a player, in favour of the number of stops between guess and answer along the network, computed by treating TfL's route data as a graph. That choice makes the feedback a graph metric and the game a search problem on the network, which is the whole subject of this report. He also built the data pipeline that snapshots stations and routes from the TfL API; the station-merging conventions in §3 follow it, so that distances here agree with the game's. The service-information panel analysed in §5 and the aggregate statistics analysed in §§7–8 are likewise his work; the latter come from the game's own analytics dashboard.

The solver (src/solver.rs) is short. Its interest lies in the analysis: why a one-step greedy rule is so close to optimal on this graph, which openers are best and why, what the progressive clues are worth, and where human play loses its efficiency. Every number in this report is computed from the network on the page or in a build step from the same code, and every figure is interactive. Click a station on any map to make it the subject of every linked figure.

2Model

Definition 1 (network). $G=(V,E)$ is a connected undirected graph with a labelling $\lambda : E \to 2^{\Lambda}\setminus\{\varnothing\}$ of the lines running each hop. $L(v) = \bigcup_{e\ni v}\lambda(e)$. $Z(v)\subset\mathbb{N}$. $d$ is the shortest-path metric of $G$ with unit edge weights.
Definition 2 (feedback). $f(g,a) = \big(d(g,a),\ \zeta(g,a),\ L(g)\cap L(a)\big)$ with $\zeta(g,a)=\textsf{exact}$ if $Z(g)\cap Z(a)\ne\varnothing$ or $Z(g)=Z(a)=\varnothing$; $\textsf{near}$ if $\exists z\in Z(g): z\pm1\in Z(a)$; $\textsf{none}$ otherwise. The guess wins iff $d(g,a)=0$.
Definition 3 (clues). The game reveals $\kappa_0(a)$, one of the answer's modes, before the first guess, and $\kappa_j(a)$ after the $j$-th wrong guess for $j=1,\dots,5$: fare zone, role, one of its lines, stops from that line's end, initial letter. Appendix B gives exact definitions.
Definition 4 (version space). After guesses $g_1..g_t$ with observations $\varphi_i$, of which $w$ were wrong, and revealed clues $c_0..c_w$, $$C_t = \{a \in V : f(g_i,a)=\varphi_i\ \forall i\le t\}\ \cap\ \bigcap_{j=0}^{w}\{a : \kappa_j(a) = c_j\}.$$ The prior on the answer is uniform on $V$.
Lemma 1 (exactness). If the solver's $G$ equals the game's, then $a\in C_t$ for all $t$, the sequence $|C_t|$ is non-increasing, and the posterior on $C_t$ is uniform.
The answer satisfies every observation and clue by definition, so it survives every intersection. Intersections only remove elements. The posterior is the prior restricted to the event $\{a\in C_t\}$, and the prior is uniform.
Lemma 2 (trilateration). For any guesses $g_1,g_2$ and any candidate $a$, $|d(g_1,a)-d(g_2,a)| \le d(g_1,g_2)$. Consequently two observations $d_1,d_2$ are jointly consistent only if $|d_1-d_2|\le d(g_1,g_2)$, and the candidates consistent with both lie in the intersection of two metric spheres $S(g_1,d_1)\cap S(g_2,d_2)$, which is small when $g_1$ and $g_2$ are far apart relative to $d_1,d_2$.
$d$ is a metric, so the triangle inequality gives $d(g_1,a)\le d(g_1,g_2)+d(g_2,a)$ and symmetrically. The second claim restates the first for the observed values.

Lemma 2 is why the solver's second guess is usually far from its first. A second guess near the first produces distances that differ by at most a few stops for every candidate, so it repeats information the first already gave. Exhibit 3 shows the spheres $S(g,d)$ directly: each colour band of the distance map is one of them.

Prior. The uniform prior is exact for the game's practice mode, which draws uniformly from all stations except the next seven daily answers, and deliberately ignorant for daily puzzles, which follow a curated schedule. The solver never reads the schedule or the game's difficulty tiers.

3The network and methods

Construction. tubele-solver fetch (src/network.rs) builds $G$ from two TfL Unified API endpoints per line: Line/{id}/StopPoints for stations and zones, and Line/{id}/Route/Sequence/all for ordered stop sequences, whose consecutive pairs become edges labelled with the line. Stop points merge into one station when their cleaned names are equal after normalisation and they lie within 600 m; the two Edgware Roads and the two Hammersmiths never merge. Zones parse every integer in TfL's zone string, so 2+3 and 2/3 both give $\{2,3\}$. The graph is checked for connectivity and embedded at compile time.

Distances. All-pairs hop distances come from one breadth-first search per source, $O(n(n+|E|))$ time and an $n\times n$ byte matrix, $$ entries. The diameter is , which matches the diameter the game's own build reports for its independently built graph; agreement on this extremal statistic over all $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs is strong evidence the graphs coincide.

Consistency under drift. The solver's graph and the game's are built independently and TfL changes routes, so the solver replaces the consistency indicator with a penalty and keeps its minimisers: $$\rho(v) = \sum_{i\le t}\Big(|d(g_i,v)-d_i| + 3[\zeta(g_i,v)\ne\zeta_i] + 3\,\big|(L(g_i)\cap L(v))\,\triangle\,S_i\big|\Big) + 3\sum_{j\le w}[\kappa_j(v)\ne c_j],\qquad C_t=\arg\min_v \rho(v).$$ When some station is fully consistent the minimum is $0$ and this is Definition 4 exactly. Otherwise the solver degrades gracefully to the stations that disagree least, and its interface says so. In every live game played for this report the minimum was $0$.

4Choosing a guess

For a candidate set $C$ and any station $g$ (candidate or not), write $p_B = |B|/|C|$ for the blocks $B\in\pi_g(C)$, the probability under the uniform posterior that the answer falls in $B$. Three summaries of the partition matter:

$$H_1(g) = -\sum_B p_B\log_2 p_B,\qquad H_2(g) = -\log_2\sum_B p_B^2,\qquad \operatorname{worst}(g)=\max_{B\ne W_g}|B|.$$
Theorem 1 (the greedy objective is collision probability). Let $C'$ be the version space after guessing $g$, counting a correct guess as leaving $0$ candidates. Then $$\mathbb{E}\,|C'| \;=\; \sum_{B\ne W_g}\frac{|B|^2}{|C|} \;=\; |C|\,2^{-H_2(g)} - \frac{[g\in C]}{|C|}.$$
The answer lies in block $B$ with probability $|B|/|C|$, and then $C'=B$, except for the win block, which leaves nothing. So $\mathbb{E}|C'| = \sum_{B\ne W_g}(|B|/|C|)|B|$. Adding and subtracting the win block, of size $1$ when $g\in C$, gives $\sum_B|B|^2/|C| - [g\in C]/|C| = |C|\sum_B p_B^2 - [g\in C]/|C|$, and $\sum_B p_B^2 = 2^{-H_2}$ by definition.

Minimising $\mathbb{E}|C'|$ is therefore, up to the win correction, maximising Rényi-2 entropy, the negative log of the probability that two independent draws from the posterior land in the same block. Since $H_2\le H_1$ with equality only for uniform partitions, the collision objective penalises a single large block more heavily than Shannon entropy does: a partition with one block of 40 and a hundred singletons has high $H_1$ but leaves a 40-candidate tail with substantial probability. Expected search depth is driven by exactly those tails, because a block of size $k$ costs roughly $\log_b k$ further guesses in a game with branching $b$ and the expectation weights it by $k/|C|$. The win correction is what lets a candidate guess, which might end the game, compete fairly with a non-candidate that splits better.

Policy. solver::rank computes, for each unguessed station, the partition of $C$ by feedback in one pass with a hash map, and orders stations by $\mathbb{E}|C'|$ ascending, then candidates before non-candidates, then $H_1$ descending, then index. For $|C|\le2$ it guesses a candidate. One decision costs $\Theta(n|C|)$ feedback evaluations; the first, with $C=V$, costs $n^2$.

Lower bounds

Proposition 1 (information bound). Let $b=\max_{g}|\pi_g(V)\setminus\{W_g\}|$ be the largest number of non-win blocks any single guess induces on $V$. Any policy identifies at most $1+b+\dots+b^{t-1}$ answers within $t$ guesses, so $$\mathbb{E}[T]\ \ge\ \frac1n\sum_{t\ge1} t\cdot\Big(\min\big(n,\textstyle\sum_{s\lt t}b^s\big)-\min\big(n,\sum_{s\lt t-1}b^s\big)\Big).$$ On this network $b=$, giving $\mathbb{E}[T]\ge $.
A decision tree in which each internal node has at most $b$ non-win children and one win leaf has at most $b^{t-1}$ nodes at depth $t$, each resolving at most one answer by a correct guess. Partitions of a subset have no more blocks than partitions of the whole set, so $b$ bounds every node. Filling depths greedily minimises $\sum_a T(a)$ subject to these caps.

This is the $\log_{|\Phi_{\text{eff}}|}n$ bound made exact for small depths: with $b=$ effective feedback values, $\log_b n = $, so most answers need at least three guesses. It ignores that blocks after the first guess are small and unevenly split, which the next bound accounts for.

Proposition 2 (second-order counting bound). Fix an opener $g$ and let $\mathcal B=\pi_g(V)\setminus\{W_g\}$. For any policy opening with $g$, each block $B\in\mathcal B$ contributes at most one answer found on guess 2 and at most $$u_3(B)=\min\Big(|B|,\ \max_{h\in V}\big([h\in B]+|\pi_h(B)\setminus\{W_h\}|\big)\Big)$$ answers found within three guesses, so $$\mathbb{E}[T]\ \ge\ \mathrm{LB}(g)=\frac1n\Big(1+\sum_{B\in\mathcal B}\big(2+3(u_3(B)-1)+4(|B|-u_3(B))\big)\Big).$$
Within $B$ the second guess $h$ is one station and finds at most $[h\in B]$ answers; each non-win block of $\pi_h(B)$ is resolved on guess 3 for at most one of its members. Maximising over $h$ separately for the two counts relaxes the requirement that one $h$ serves both, so the cap holds for every policy, and filling the remaining answers at the smallest feasible depths minimises the total.

The exact optimum, and why greedy is enough

The optimal expected number of guesses satisfies the Bellman recursion $$V^*(C)=1+\min_{g\in V}\sum_{B\in\pi_g(C),\,B\ne W_g}\frac{|B|}{|C|}\,V^*(B),\qquad V^*(\{a\})=1.$$ Its state space is the set of reachable candidate sets, its branching is $n$ per state, and constructing optimal identification trees is NP-hard in general (Hyafil and Rivest, 1976). Exact solutions are feasible for games of Wordle's size with careful pruning (Bertsimas and Paskov), and greedy policies of this kind carry a general $O(\log n)$ approximation guarantee for expected cost (Dasgupta, 2005; Golovin and Krause, 2011). Here the counting bounds make a direct argument unnecessary: the gap between the greedy policy and $\min_g \mathrm{LB}(g)$ is guesses on the map alone, and between the clue-aware policy and Proposition 3 it is . Any further optimisation is bounded by these gaps.

5The service-information panel

The live game shows a panel headed service information: the answer's mode from the start, then one more clue after each wrong guess. Each clue is a function of the answer alone, a partition of $V$ fixed in advance. Appendix B defines them; two choose among several values by the game's stable string hash $h_0=7$, $h_{i+1}=(31h_i+c_i)\bmod 2^{32}$ over UTF-16 code units, taking element $h\bmod k$ of a sorted list. Since each $\kappa_j$ is deterministic, its mutual information with the answer under the uniform prior is its entropy, $I(\kappa_j;A)=H(\kappa_j)$.

Exhibit 9What each clue is worth

Information in each clue alone, computed on the solver's graph. The six together carry of the bits needed and single out stations by themselves. The stops-from-the-end clue is the most informative single clue, because it is a distance in its own right.

The clue for the next wrong guess arrives whichever station is guessed, so the right objective for that guess is the joint partition $\pi_g(C)\wedge\kappa_{w+1}$. Both implementations apply clues to the version space but score guesses on $\pi_g(C)$ alone. Scoring on the joint partition is a refinement whose possible gain is bounded by the gap to Proposition 3.

The model is a poor description of the solver, whose candidate sets collapse faster than geometrically once the second guess lands; for the solver the more meaningful figure is $\log_2 n/\mathbb{E}[T]$, also tabulated. With caveats: the dashboard counts finished games only, so abandoned games are missing; players are a self-selected sample; daily answers are curated; and the model assumes a uniform answer within a geometrically shrinking set, which ignores that people do not maintain exact version spaces. Within those caveats, the solver obtains nearly twice as much information from each guess as a typical player.

The reason is not the choice of opener, which people get right, but what happens after it. The shared-line component is a set intersection over up to five lines, and combining it with a distance and a zone reading across a 420-station map, then choosing a second guess that trilaterates (Lemma 2), is the work the version space does without effort.

8How people play

This section treats the game's dashboard as a behavioural dataset and asks what it reveals about the strategies of the people who generate it. The data are aggregates: counts by guess index, by distance band, by cohort, by hour and by outcome, for 9,550 daily and 4,522 practice games started, with no per-player traces and no answers. Each hypothesis below is stated before the evidence, tested against it with the statistic named, and compared with the solver's behaviour on the same graph where a comparison is meaningful. Two-sample comparisons of proportions use the pooled two-proportion $z$-test; intervals are Wilson 95% intervals. With samples in the thousands, almost every difference is significant, so effect sizes are what carry the argument.

8.1 Anchoring on salient hubs

Hypothesis B1 (salience). Players choose openers by familiarity rather than by information, so openers concentrate on a few prominent Zone 1 interchanges far beyond what opener quality warrants.
Exhibit 18Who opens where, and how good it is

The fifteen most common openers, by share of all first guesses (bars), with the solver's mean guesses from that opener (dots, right panel; dashed: the best opener, dotted: the mean over all openers). A uniform choice would give each station 0.24% of opens.

The evidence supports B1 in its first half and refutes it in its second. Openers are extremely concentrated, but the concentration falls on stations that are also good openers: weighted by use, the listed openers average guesses under the solver's policy against for a random opener, which places the typical human opener in the best of all stations. Salience and quality coincide on this network because the busiest interchanges are also the best-connected vertices of the graph. Anchoring costs players little at the first guess.

8.2 Do players use the feedback?

Hypothesis B2 (partial use). Players respond to the distance reading, moving closer to the answer on each guess, but do not maintain the full version space; their distance to the answer therefore falls slowly and roughly linearly, where a consistent player's collapses after two guesses.
Exhibit 19Distance to the answer, guess by guess

Mean stops between each guess and the answer, by guess number, over the games that reach that guess (a correct guess counts as 0). The solver's curves use uniform answers; the daily curve sits lower partly because daily answers are more central (8.4).

The pattern in Exhibit 19 is the signature of B2. Daily players' mean distance falls from 6.22 stops at the first guess to 5.24, 4.31 and 3.53, then stalls at 3.55 on the fifth guess and reaches only 3.07 on the sixth; practice players fall from 8.84 to 4.98 over six guesses and are flat from the fourth guess on. The solver, after the same first reading, is at 5.7 stops on its second guess (it chooses for information, not proximity, per Lemma 2) and at 0.3 on its third, by which point almost every game is over. A player who intersected the constraints exactly would also see the distance collapse; a slow linear decline means each guess is being moved towards the answer by the distance reading alone, a hill-climbing strategy, while the line and zone components are largely discarded.

8.3 The service-information panel

Hypothesis B3 (clues help, unevenly). The revealed clues raise the chance of solving, most of all the first letter, which is the clue a person can apply without a map.

The dashboard reports the number of clues shown at the time of each win. Because one clue is revealed per wrong guess, clues shown equals guesses minus one for every win, so this distribution is the solve distribution shifted by one and cannot separate the effect of clues from the effect of having had more guesses. The data identify only one contrast: the first letter arrives before the sixth guess, and the hazard jumps there, from to in daily play and from to in practice, far above the trend of the preceding guesses. The first letter carries 4.10 bits on its own (Exhibit 9) and, unlike the distance clues, can be checked against memory rather than a map. A last-chance effect, players taking more care on the final guess, would produce a jump in the same place, so the jump is consistent with B3 but does not prove it.

8.4 Familiarity and the curated schedule

Hypothesis B4 (familiarity). People find unfamiliar stations harder for reasons that have nothing to do with the structure of the network.

The game assigns each daily answer a tier: core, standard or deep. The tier of individual stations is not used here; only the aggregate win rates are. Players win of core puzzles and of deep ones (), and need 3.74 against 4.42 guesses on wins. The solver's difficulty runs the other way. Grouping answers by zone as a proxy for remoteness, the solver needs guesses for Zone 1 answers and for Zone 7 and beyond: outer stations sit on long branches where a single distance reading isolates them. The contrast supports B4. What makes a station hard for people is that they do not know it, not that the network hides it.

The same data expose the curation of daily answers. From King's Cross, the most common opener, a uniformly drawn answer is on average stops away. Practice players opening there see 7.62, within a fraction of a stop of the uniform figure, as practice's uniform draw predicts. Daily players opening there see 6.07, so daily answers are some stops nearer the centre than uniform. This is a property of the schedule, not of play, and it is the main reason daily and practice statistics differ.

Limitations of this section. Every figure here is an aggregate over games or devices; nothing links a guess to the player who made it, so individual strategies are inferred from population curves and could be produced by mixtures of very different players. Devices stand in for people. Daily answers are curated and their tiers are assigned by the game, so daily comparisons mix player behaviour with puzzle difficulty. Players are self-selected, returning players doubly so. Abandoned games ( of daily and of practice games started) are absent from the finished-game statistics, which therefore flatter players slightly. The hour of day is in London time and play from other time zones blurs it.

9Discussion

Three properties of the network explain why a one-step rule is near-optimal. Feedback is rich: a single guess from a good opener induces about 150 blocks, so most of the work happens in the first two guesses, where greedy and optimal policies differ least. Blocks after the first guess are small, mostly under ten, and for small blocks the counting bound is nearly tight. And the metric structure (Lemma 2) means good second guesses are plentiful: almost any station far from the first guess trilaterates.

The panel matters more than any choice of opener. Knowing the mode removes 1.5 bits before the first guess, and each later clue refines a block the feedback has already made small. Together they move the expected game from 2.71 guesses to about 2.25 and the worst case from five to four or fewer.

10Limitations and threats to validity

Map drift. The solver's graph is rebuilt from TfL Open Data and may disagree with the game's after route changes. The matching diameter and zero-penalty live games suggest agreement today, and the soft-consistency rule (§3) bounds the damage of a disagreement, but the results in §6 are for the solver's graph. Prior. All results assume uniform answers; daily answers are curated and Exhibit 17 shows they differ. Greedy versus optimal. The gaps to the lower bounds are upper bounds on the possible improvement, not measurements of it; the true optimum may lie between. Two implementations. The reference Rust solver and the JavaScript re-implementation used for the figures agree exactly for the best opener on the map alone and to within 0.01 guesses for the others, where ties in $H_1$ are broken by floating-point summation order; with clues they differ slightly (2.264 against ), from differences in how clues enter the candidate set and tie-breaking, and both figures are reported. Human data. Dashboard counts are aggregates of finished games from self-selected players; there is no per-game data, so per-guess human behaviour is modelled, not observed.

11Implementation

The solver is one Rust binary (src/main.rs, network.rs, solver.rs, tui.rs, site.rs). A ratatui terminal interface draws the network in Braille in line colours, zooming to the surviving candidates. In automatic mode it drives the live game in a headless Chromium (site.rs): it types each guess, chooses the matching suggestion, reads the rendered feedback row and the service-information panel, and nothing else; requests to the game's analytics endpoint are blocked so automated games stay out of the statistics above. Releases build macOS and static Linux binaries in CI and publish them to a Homebrew tap and as release assets for mise.

BAppendix: clue definitions

Mode. The station's modes sorted (dlr, elizabeth, overground, tube); element $h(\text{name})\bmod k$. Fare zone. $Z(a)$, or outside the zones. On the network. End of the line if $a$ has degree 1 in $G$; else an interchange if $|L(a)|>1$; else a through station. One of its lines. The station's line identifiers sorted; element $h(\text{name})\bmod k$. From the end of the line. On the subgraph of edges carrying that line, a multi-source breadth-first search from the line's ends, the vertices with at most one distinct neighbour on it; if the line has no ends, the minimum over the station's lines. First letter. The first character of the name.

CAppendix: data pipeline and reproducibility

The network is rebuilt with tubele-solver fetch. The map-only results in §6 come from tubele-solver bench and tubele-solver openers; the live behaviour from cargo test -- --ignored, which plays the daily game and a practice round through the browser. The figures here are computed by build/build.js beside this page from data/network.json, using a port of the solver (build/solver.js) whose map-only results match the Rust binary up to floating-point tie-breaking.
